## What works today

BIMCraft is in early, fast development. Honest status:

| Area | Works today |
|---|---|
| Datums | Levels with elevations, each a plane over its extent (a rectangle in plan; by default the model's, a metre past it): sections and elevations draw a level where they see its extent, and the 3D view outlines its plane (its far two edges, all four when selected), hidden behind what's in front, its name lettered on a head past the far edges' right end, a click on either picking it; selected, its ends in a section or an elevation and the middles of its plane's edges in 3D are grips that drag its extent; grids that name themselves 1, 2, 3… or A, B, C… |
| Walls | Chains of walls, straight or curved (each turning `arc` degrees), on any of Revit's six location lines (the wall's or its core's centre line, a finish or a core face, exterior or interior); compound types with layers (structure, substrate, thermal, membrane, finish) from the exterior in, the exterior being to the left of start to end, so walls drawn clockwise face out; joins at any angle, mitred, three or more walls sharing a node, a wall ending on another stopped at its face, and sharp corners squared off; base level and offset; the top at a level or an unconnected height (by default, up to the next level), or attached to the underside of a roof or a floor (a base, onto one), following it where it's over the wall; flip, as Revit's arrows do |
| Doors, windows | Hosted in walls: place one at a point on the wall (`at`), or by wall and offset; marks number themselves, on in the series they are in (`P1`, `P2` → `P3`; `J-09` → `J-10`) and kept through IFC as the doors' and windows' tags; flip facing and hand; refused when they don't fit their wall or overlap. Of one of the project's own types (a width, a height, a sill), or of a family of their own: a new project comes with a single door, a double door, a sliding door, a fixed window, a casement window and a sliding window, each saying how it opens (swing, double swing, sliding, double sliding, folding or fixed, which IFC's operation types and the schedules carry), and more load from `.json` files. A family's forms (frame, leaf, handle, sashes, panes) are drawn in the opening, its `Width` and `Height` size the hole in the wall, its `Sill Height` is a window's sill, its formulas read `Wall Thickness`, and its plan symbols (a door's swing) go with it as it flips; sections show them beyond their line, never cut |
| Floors | A boundary you give, open through any openings inside it, or the outline of the walls on the level, open over each loop of them standing free inside, a courtyard (a loop joined to the outer walls, or with a room in it, is rooms, and floored over); the top at the level or a height offset from it, or sloped along a slope arrow (from its tail, where it's at the offset, rising so many degrees toward its head, as Revit's slope arrow does), as thick as its type across the slope; walls attached to one follow it; in plan, its openings, the slope arrow and its slope |
| Ceilings | In the room around a point, out to its walls' faces (Revit's automatic ceiling) and open round any walls standing free inside it (a courtyard's loop, a core), or along a boundary, open through any openings inside it; their underside 2600 mm up by default, their type's thickness over it; types with square tiles, which the reflected ceiling plan draws, stopping at the openings |
| Massing | A mass is an outline extruded from a level (and an offset) up to the next level, a level or a height, as Revit's conceptual mass is, in its own category (`mass`) that Visibility/Graphics hides (Show Mass, MH, does it for a view in one step): dashed in plan where the cut plane passes through it and in a section where the line cuts it (outlined, not filled), a see-through solid in 3D. Give it a `top` outline with as many points as its footprint (twin for twin, the same way round) and it is lofted straight between the two, or a `lean` (the footprint moved sideways for its top: a sloped mass); the plan then draws the outline at the height it cuts, the section the line where it cuts the skin, 3D a closed skin of triangles, and area, perimeter and volume, per level too, come from the cross sections between the outlines, exactly. `mass_floors` makes a floor at each level it crosses, outlined as the mass is there; the floors hold their mass and level, so running it again updates them instead of doubling them (`prune` deletes the ones on levels the mass no longer crosses; without it they are listed as `stale`). `wall_by_face` (on faces that stand plumb), `floor_by_face` and `roof_by_face` (on the top outline) make a wall on each edge, and a floor and a roof on its top face, and remember their mass and face: `update_by_face` (UF) rebuilds them from the mass as it is now, as Revit's Update to Face does, and refuses the lot, naming each, when a face is gone. The `massfloor` schedule has a row for each level each mass crosses (floor area, perimeter, and the volume up to the next level or the mass's top, to sum into gross floor area and volume); the `mass` schedule one row for each mass. IFC writes a mass as a building element proxy of object type `Mass` (a lofted one as a closed face set, with a `BIMCraft_Mass` property set that says how its corners pair up), and reads one back; a mass with a courtyard in it, or whose outline changes from base to top with no such set, is left out and listed. Masses take the project parameters, phases and design options as other elements do, and move, copy, mirror and rotate with them. What a mass made is ordinary in a copy, after a split, in IFC and once its mass is deleted |
| Shaft openings | A hole along an outline from a level (and an offset) up to the next level, a level or a height, cut through every floor, ceiling and roof it reaches into: in 3D, sections, areas and IFC; in plan, its outline and a cross where the plan cuts it, and the hole in a floor seen below |
| Columns, stairs, roofs | Columns of rectangular or round types, turned to any angle, from their level up to the next (or a height, or a level); cut in plan, in poché. Stairs from a start point toward an end point, in as many risers as keep each at most 180 mm, treads 280 mm deep, up to the next level: one straight run, or runs with a landing between each two (as deep as the stair is wide) after so many risers, the next run going on straight, turning left or right, or coming back beside the last; in plan, their risers up to the cut line and the rest dashed, landings over the cut plane dashed, and the walk line marked UP (DN from the level above). Roofs on a level and an offset from it, by footprint: flat, or over any outline every edge sloping up at one slope, or at a slope of its own, until the slopes meet, in hips, valleys and ridges (its straight skeleton), but the edges left upright as gable ends; round courtyards too, holes in the footprint whose edges are eaves like the outline's (or gables); by default over the walls of the level below, 500 mm past their faces, at 30°, and open over each loop of them standing free inside, a courtyard (a loop joined to the outer walls, or with a room in it, is rooms, and roofed over). Or by extrusion: a profile drawn along a line, swept across. Openings through them, and a roof that runs into another (a dormer's) ends where it meets that one's top. Dormers: walls standing on a roof's slope under a gable roof of their own, with a window in front, the roof open under them. In plan, their ridges, hips and valleys, and from the level below their edge dashed overhead |
| Rooms | Placed at a point, bounded by the walls and room separation lines around it, and following them when they change; free-standing islands (a shaft) are subtracted; area and perimeter at the wall centre line, or at the walls' faces (`room_area`, as Revit's Area and Volume Computations); IFC spaces carry `Qto_SpaceBaseQuantities`, gross at the centre lines and net at the faces; Revit's Upper Limit, Limit Offset and Base Offset, with Unbounded Height and Volume; frozen rooms keep their boundary and are never recalculated; walls and columns bound rooms while their Room Bounding is on, at the level's Computation Height; two rooms can't share a space (the later is redundant, unbounded); unbounded rooms sit as a 1 m square; Rooms > Reference (the cross) and Room Separation Lines are subcategories; rooms show in 3D as see-through solids when selected, or all of them when a 3D view shows the category |
| Ramps, railings | Ramps from a start point toward an end point at a slope (8.33%, 1 in 12, by default) up to a level or a height, in one run or turning at landings as stairs do, solid down to their level; in plan, their outline, dashed over the cut plane, and the walk line. Railings down a side of a stair or a ramp, 50 mm in from its edge, climbing with it and going wherever it goes, or along a path on a level; a rail at their type's height over what they run along, and balusters their type's spacing apart; in plan, their line, dashed over the cut plane |
| Structure | Beams, structural columns, isolated footings, wall (strip) footings and structural floors, Revit's Structure tab on the design in [docs/disciplines.md](docs/disciplines.md). A beam, or a wall footing, is a **run**: a straight element from one point to another, of a type with a section (`width` across by `depth` deep, or round) made of a material of the library, at a height over its level (the height of its top, middle or bottom line at each end, so it can slope up to 75°), in a named `system`; a chain of points makes one for each step. A structural column is a column of its own category and types; an isolated footing is a column laid down, its top at the level by default and as thick as `thickness`. A floor says it is `structural`. In plan a run hangs under its level (seen from it, with its centre line dashed to pick it by), crosses the cut plane (in poché) or lies overhead (dashed); a section cuts a rectangular run across its section, and the 3D view draws a sloped prism or, round, a tube; snaps find their ends and middles. Each has a schedule (Id, Type, Level, Length, Width, Depth, with System to group a schedule by), a place in Visibility/Graphics, project parameters and phases, and its IFC class: IfcBeam, IfcColumn, IfcFooting (`PAD_FOOTING`, `STRIP_FOOTING`) and IfcSlab, each with `LoadBearing` in its `Pset_*Common`, swept along its own axis; IFC import reads them back, taking a column or slab whose `Pset_*Common` says it carries load as structural |
| Plumbing | Pipes, plumbing fixtures, pipe fittings and pipe accessories, Revit's Plumbing & Piping panel on the same **run** the Structure tab uses. A pipe is round (its `width` its outside diameter) in PVC or copper, at a height over its level at each end (a drain falls, a riser stands upright: the same point twice and two heights), its line the middle unless told, in a named `system` (Água fria, Esgoto) that IFC carries as an `IfcSystem` its pipes are assigned to. A section cuts a pipe in a ring (the poché of a circle, an ellipse when the line is oblique). A fixture is a component of a family whose category says what it is: the project starts with a basin, a WC, a bathtub, a kitchen sink, a shower, a floor drain, elbows, tees and a gate valve, original drawings in the project's language, written to IFC as `IfcSanitaryTerminal`, `IfcWasteTerminal` (`FLOORTRAP`), `IfcPipeFitting` and `IfcValve` and read back into a family of the same category. A family says which fixture or fitting it is (`kind`: `wc`, `lav`, `sink`, `laundry`, `shower`, `bath`…, `floor_drain`, `bend`, `tee`…), which IFC writes as its predefined type (a WC `TOILETPAN`, a basin `WASHHANDBASIN`, an elbow `BEND`) and the import reads back; the starter families say theirs. Fittings and valves are centred on their pipe, so placing one at the pipe's height lines them up; there are no connectors yet |
| Electrical | Conduits, cable trays, wires, distribution boards, outlets and switches and luminaires, Revit's Electrical panel on the same run and the same fixtures. A conduit (eletroduto) and a wire are round, a cable tray (eletrocalha) a width by a depth, each at a height over its level at each end (a riser stands upright), in a named `system` (a circuit: Circuito 3) that IFC carries as an `IfcSystem`. The project starts with PVC and steel conduits, perforated trays in three sizes and copper wires, and a distribution board (12, 24 and 36 circuits), a ceiling and a recessed luminaire, an outlet and a switch, each at the mounting height its type says (an outlet's 300, 1100 or 2100 mm to its centre; a luminaire hung from the slab), original drawings in the project's language, the luminaires, outlets and switches carrying connectors (voltage, poles, a load for each type, per phase on a three-phase outlet; `inspect` warns of a device without one), written as `IfcCableCarrierSegment` (`CONDUITSEGMENT`, `CABLETRAYSEGMENT`; a `CABLELADDERSEGMENT` from another tool is read as a tray), `IfcCableSegment`, `IfcElectricDistributionBoard`, `IfcOutlet`, `IfcSwitchingDevice` and `IfcLightFixture`, and read back. The project also keeps Revit's Electrical Settings: voltages (each with the band a device may read within), distribution systems (phases, wires and the voltages each supplies) and wire types (material, insulation, temperature and an ampacity for each section, always in mm²), through `voltage`, `distribution_system` and `wire_type`; it starts with 230/400 V, 127/220 V, 220/380 V and 277/480 V, and copper wires of the NBR 5410 tables (PVC 70 °C, XLPE or EPR 90 °C) and of the NEC (THHN, THW; AWG sizes kept in mm²), whose ampacities are the contributor's and unchecked. Manage > Electrical Settings edits all three in one dialog, applied as one undo step. |
| Lightning protection (SPDA) | Air terminals (captores), down conductors (condutores de descida), earthing electrodes (hastes de aterramento), inspection boxes and earthing conductors (the ring or mesh), the vocabulary of NBR 5419; Revit has no such tab, so its tools are BIMCraft's own, on the same runs and fixtures. A down conductor is a run that stands upright down a wall (the same point twice and two heights), round in steel or copper or a flat strip; an earthing conductor runs under the level; both belong to a named system. The project starts with a Franklin air terminal in three heights, an earthing rod driven 2.4 or 3 m and a round inspection box, original drawings in the project's language. IFC4 has no lightning protection, so the terminals, rods and boxes are `IfcBuildingElementProxy` of object type `LightningTerminal`, `EarthingRod` and `InspectionBox`, which a reader can filter on and BIMCraft reads back into a family of its category; the conductors are `IfcCableSegment` `CONDUCTORSEGMENT`, told apart by a `Category` BIMCraft writes. A conductor under the level shows in a plan whose view range reaches it (its View Depth) |
| Data (lógica, dados e voz) | Data outlets, voice outlets, racks, patch panels, the cable trays of the Electrical tab and structured cabling, the vocabulary of NBR 14565. Cabling is a run like a wire, round, in a named `system` (a link, a network), a Cat6, a Cat6A or an optical cable to start from. The project starts with a data and a voice outlet (a plate with its jacks cut in), a patch panel and a floor rack in three sizes, each at the height its type says, original drawings in the project's language, written as `IfcOutlet` (`DATAOUTLET`, `TELEPHONEOUTLET`), `IfcCommunicationsAppliance` (`NETWORKAPPLIANCE`) and, for the rack, which IFC4 has no class for, an `IfcBuildingElementProxy` of object type `Rack`, and read back into a family of the same category (an `IfcOutlet` of another type is read as a power outlet) |
| Reinforcement | `rebar` makes reinforcing bars: a round bar of a diameter (3 to 100 mm) and a steel grade (CA-50 unless told) bent along points in mm, in the beam, column, footing or floor it reinforces, which it goes with; `closed` makes a hoop, and `count` with `step` a layer of bars or hoops along a beam. A schedule of rebar lists grade, diameter, length, count and weight (steel at 7850 kg/m³) and, grouped, is a bar schedule. IFC writes and reads each as an `IfcReinforcingBar`, aggregated to its host. The 3D view draws them as tubes (inside their host, so hide it to see them), a section draws the bars it cuts as dots over the poché, and a plan draws each bar of its level as a thin line (solid under the cut plane, dashed over it and under the view range's top, a bar that stands as a dot, bars lying on one another drawn once), hidden with the category. `reinforce` arms a beam, a column, a footing or a floor (round columns and footings, and sloped floors, too) by rules: the engineer gives the cover (or the host's `Cobrimento` parameter) and how many bars, how thick and how far apart (a beam's bars below and above, stirrups with closer ends and hooks, skin bars and anchors; a column's bars on each face, stirrups and ties; a footing's two layers of mesh with their ends turned up; a floor's meshes below and above, stopping short of its outline and openings), and it lays them out inside the cover, checks they fit with room between them and says what to change; a beam's bars and a column's end in hooks if told, some of a beam's bottom bars bend up at each end to the top at an angle (`bent`) and a column's bars step in toward its axis near the top (`jog`); each bar is on a layer, and reinforcing again replaces what it made and leaves bars drawn by hand. `hosts` arms a list alike, all or none (up to 1000 hosts and 20000 bars), and `reinforcement_preset` keeps a named set of the parameters for a kind of host in the project, which `reinforce` takes with `preset` and lets what is given replace. The numbers are the engineer's; `check` "NBR 6118" (with the aggressiveness class and `fck`) adds notes, never a refusal, where the bars made fall short of its usual limits (cover, bar sizes and spacings, stirrups, least and most steel, skin bars), typed from public handbooks and not from the standard's text, so check one against a copy before relying on it. On the desktop, the Structure tab's Reinforce (RF) opens a dialog for the elements selected (any number, of one kind), with the fields of their kind, saying a refusal inside itself, keeping what was typed for the next of its kind, saving the fields as a preset to choose for another and deleting one, and with Remove Bars for the bars it made. Not yet: bends other than a hook, a bent-up bar and a column's jog, round beams, bars beyond a section's cut seen through the concrete. |
| Types | Wall, floor, ceiling, door, window, column, structural column, footing, beam, strip footing, pipe, conduit, cable tray, wire, down conductor, earthing conductor, cable, roof and railing types; editing one changes every element that uses it (a run type's roundness and material too) |
| Materials | A library of materials in each project, as Revit's Material Browser: each with a shading colour, a cut pattern and a surface pattern (solid, diagonal, crosshatch, horizontal, vertical, grid, brick, concrete or insulation), drawn in code. Wall, floor, roof and ceiling types are built of layers, each a function, a material from the library and a thickness of its own, adding up to the type's: a wall's from the exterior in, the others' from the top down. Plans and sections cut each layer and hatch it with its material's cut pattern where it's wide enough to hold it (a drafting pattern, the same on screen and on paper); a floor's top in plan and a ceiling's underside in its ceiling plan show their surface pattern at its size in the building; the 3D view shades each layer in its material's colour. A material may have a texture over its colour in the 3D view: a pattern drawn in code (brick, block, boards, concrete, plaster, tile, roof tiles, fabric, metal or insulation, each at its real size) or a picture you add (a PNG or a JPEG, kept in the project, at most 1024 px a side, up to 24 of them), laid on every face of what's made of the material at the size you give it, turned and moved as you say, by the face's own axes (across a wall, a slab's east and north); the library's materials come with theirs. Paint (PT) lays a material, with its texture, over one face of an element, a wall's exterior or a floor's top, over what its type is made of, as Revit's Paint does. A material, with its picture, can be kept in your Favorites, a file of your own (`favorites.json` in your configuration folder, or where `BIMCRAFT_FAVORITES` says) that every project reads; textures show in the 3D view alone (plans and sections keep their patterns), and IFC carries the colour |
| Editing | Modify any field, of one element or many in one step (a wall put on another location line stays where it is); move, copy, rotate and mirror, with the walls joined to them stretching to stay joined and doors and windows going with their walls (a mirrored wall runs back the other way, its exterior mirrored); trim or extend two walls to a corner; split a wall, an arc into two arcs; flip walls; delete (a wall takes its doors and windows with it, and they their tags; a roof or floor walls are attached to lets them go); undo and redo |
| Snaps and the ruler | Points picked while drawing stick to what the plan has there, as Revit's snaps do: the ends, middles and centres of walls (an arc's too), grids and components, the centres of columns, structural columns and footings with the corners and edge middles of their outlines (a round one's four quadrants), so a wall can start flush with a column's edge, and the corners of floors, ceilings, roofs and shafts; where two straight walls or grids cross; the foot of the perpendicular from the last point to a straight wall or grid; and a wall's centre line, so a wall drawn onto it meets it. It also sticks to the anchors of notes, dimensions, tags, spot elevations, section and elevation marks, detail groups and repeating details; a camera's mark gives its eye and target, never the body, lens or name it is drawn with. Each shows its own marker and its name. A direction from the last point sticks to multiples of an angle (5°, 15°, 30°, 45° or 90°; 15° by default), from east and from the line before it, so corners come out exact, sharp ones too: a dashed guide shows it, and where it crosses a wall or grid the point lands there. The line being drawn carries a ruler: graduations at round lengths, its length and its angle (at the corner it turns once there's a line before it, drawn as an arc). Manage > Snaps switches each snap and sets the angle steps, and Revit's SE, SM, SI, SC, SP and SN take one snap alone for the next point (SO none, SS back); Measure (ME) lays a ruler between two snapped points and reads its length, parts and angle; the `measure` command does the same. Each plan, section and elevation has a background grid, a main grid and a subgrid with their own spacing, divisions, colours and pens, turned by an angle, kept in the view's graphics and controlled by its template; Grid snapping (Manage > Snaps, or the grid menu in the view control bar) lands a free point on its crossings |
| Annotation | Aligned dimensions between parallel references, one segment or a chain: wall faces, centre lines and ends, door and window centres and jambs, grids; and between endpoints (a wall's ends and its faces' corners, a line's or a grid's ends, a jamb's), aligned along them or linear, horizontal or vertical, as the cursor lies or the Options Bar says (Auto, Aligned, Horizontal, Vertical; Space steps them); angular dimensions between two lines that meet, radial and diameter ones on round columns, curved walls, circles and arcs, and arc length ones along a curved wall or an arc; all measuring the model as it is, so they follow what they measure (a change that would turn a reference out of parallel with the others is refused). In a 3D view, aligned dimensions between two points or edges of the model (square to an edge), kept by the view and drawn over it, on either painter and on the sheets that hold it. A dimension's number takes a typed length and moves what it measures; locked segments show their padlocks always; Align leaves a padlock that keeps the two lines aligned after every change, refusing one the other locks can't hold with. Dimension types, as Revit's type parameters: continuous, baseline or ordinate strings, diagonal, arrow, open arrow or dot ticks, pens, colour, the dimension and witness lines' extensions, gaps and control, and the numbers' font, size, lettering, offset, background, read convention, units and rounding; a dimension's own prefix, suffix, text above and below, Replace With Text and Equality Display (EQ). Tags on any element showing its type, a door's or window's mark, a room's name or any field of its schedule, going where their doors and windows go, with or without a leader; tag everything not tagged yet in one go. Text notes in plans, sections, elevations and on sheets, at any angle, over several lines, with a leader, of a text type (font, size, bold, italic, underline, width factor, colour and a border: any font the system has, Arial by default, which a look-alike such as Liberation Sans stands in for where it isn't installed, or IBM Plex Sans, IBM Plex Mono or our single-stroke lettering; any script a font on the system has, CJK included), written and rewritten on the canvas (Shift+Enter breaks a line), and with Override Formatting, parts of them bold, italic or underlined whatever their type; spot elevations reading the top of what they stand on in plan (a floor, a roof's slope, a stair's tread), or the height of a point in a section |
| Views | A floor plan per level with Revit's view range: cut at its cut plane (1200 mm up by default), what lies below seen from it down to the bottom, faint beyond it down to the view depth, and roofs overhead up to the top: wall poché with clean joins at any angle, compound walls' layers, hatched with their materials' cut patterns where they're wide enough (else lighter where they aren't structure), door swings, window glass, room tags, grid bubbles, dimensions, door and window tags; PNG and SVG, which letter the view's name at the plan's bottom left (the window letters it too, over the canvas and on the screen only, which File > Settings can hide; a viewport on a sheet has its own title). Sections and elevations along any line: the cut in poché (a roof's along its slope, a compound wall's, floor's, roof's or ceiling's with its layers), what lies beyond in hidden line, levels named with their heights, grids; the four building elevations in one go; their marks on every plan; PNG. Crop regions on plans and sections: the view shows only what's inside, grids stopping at its edge with their bubbles. A reflected ceiling plan per level: its walls cut at 2300 mm (over the doors, through the windows), looking up at the ceilings, their tiles and the shafts through them, and at the beams and columns over the cut up to the ceilings, in projection (one over a ceiling is hidden by it). A 3D view, from any angle: walls with their doors and windows cut out, slabs and ceilings with their shafts cut out, columns, stairs and roofs, door leaves and glass, shaded (each layer in its material's colour), with an edge wherever faces meet, but none between the facets of a curve (a curved wall's, a round form's, an imported mesh's where its triangles turn less than 20°), which shades smoothly across them; PNG. A plan or a section always has a scale, 1 : n (1 : 100, or 1/8" = 1'-0" in feet and inches, until set): it stands for paper at that scale, and its annotation (lettering, grid bubbles, marks, dimension ticks) and its line weights are that paper's, so on screen a line is as thick as it would print at the zoom (at least a pixel; Thin Lines draws all of them one). A line weight is a pen, 1 to 16, or 0, a hairline, the thinnest line the screen, a PNG or a printer draws, at any scale and zoom; what pens 1 to 16 draw is the project's Line Weights (Manage > Line Weights, as Revit's): the model's pens at each of a list of scales, a view taking the nearest scale's, and the annotation's (dimensions, tags, text, grids, levels, marks) at any scale, edited by scale, added and deleted, applied as one undo step and saved with the project. Which pen each model category draws its own lines in, cut and seen, is the project's Object Styles (Manage > Object Styles, as Revit's), in every plan and section, a view's Visibility/Graphics over them. Detail lines draw in one view (a plan, a section, an elevation or a sheet) in a line style: a pen, a colour and a line pattern, its freely ordered gaps, straight strokes, dots, centered parallel offsets, waves, zigzags and text in mm on paper so it reads the same at any scale; the Line Pattern Creator previews straight or curved paths and letters text with bundled IBM Plex Sans, while geometry and dimensions keep the centerline (Manage > Line Styles, styles and patterns made, changed, renamed and deleted as one undo step; a project starts with Thin, Medium and Wide Lines, a Hairline, Hidden, Centre and Overhead Lines). A cut wall's outline is inked over its poché, and not across where walls join. A plan or a section has a detail level too: coarse (what it cuts in one poché, an opening as its gap and jambs), medium (a compound element's layers and an opening's leaf, swing and glass) or fine, as it has always drawn (the layers' patterns too); the 3D view has a visual style: shaded, hidden line (paper-white faces with their edges, what a face hides staying hidden) or wireframe (every edge, seen through the faces). A viewport takes its view's scale; a sheet keeps annotation at paper size. A view fitted into an image (a PNG, Zoom to Fit) leaves as much room round it as its annotation takes at its scale, which grows with the scale, so 1 : 500 doesn't cut its grid bubbles or level names off. A level can have more plans than its own, as Revit's Duplicate View makes them (`duplicate_view`, DP): each a plan view with its own view range, crop and graphics and its own dimensions, tags, notes and spot elevations over the same model; with detailing it starts with its source's annotation; a dependent view follows its parent's view range and graphics (scale and detail too), has a crop of its own, and shows its parent's annotation as well as its own. They go on sheets, take view templates and have ceiling plans as levels do. Sections, elevations, schedules, sheets and the 3D view aren't duplicated yet. The 3D view is parallel (axonometric) or in perspective, as through a camera: picked from Projection in its Properties (or `perspective`), seen through a lens of a focal length typed there, from an eye raised or lowered to a height typed there, and orbited, panned, zoomed, picked and edited as ever, on the processor or the graphics card. The Camera tool (`camera`) makes a view of its own, as Revit's does: an eye and a target are clicked in a plan (each at a height over it, typed in the Options Bar), and a named 3D view in perspective opens looking from the eye at the target; it is listed under 3D Views in the Project Browser, opens from its own camera, and its name, eye, target and lens are read and changed in Properties, whether it is open or its mark on the plan is picked (`camera` reads or changes one too, by name or id; a view with no camera shows the azimuth and elevation it is framed by on a sheet instead). The camera shows on its level's plan as a mark at its eye with a line to what it looks at: it is picked there and opened by its right-click menu (or a double click), and its eye and target follow the mouse as the view is orbited, panned or zoomed, so leaving the view and coming back stands it where it was left. The 3D view has a section box, as Revit's: six planes square to the axes that keep what lies between them, the model's surfaces and its point clouds alike, turned on and off with BX or its Properties, each face dragged by its grip in the view or typed in Properties, its extents kept while it's off; the levels it doesn't reach are left out.
